# Landscape: language-learning games and immersion apps

Research for a Sims-style immersion game (French or Mandarin) where the player only ever sees the target language and the game keeps them at i+1. Sources are inline links. Items marked **[unverified]** are claims I could not confirm against a primary source; **[not read]** means I could only see the abstract or a secondary summary.

## The 10 lessons that matter most for us

1. **Almost every "language game" to date is a vocabulary trainer with a world around it.** Influent, Noun Town, Shashingo, Lingotopia, Earthlingo, Drops: click object, hear word, quiz later. Reviewers consistently say they are pleasant but "won't teach you to speak" ([Bonus Stage on Influent](https://www.bonusstage.co.uk/tag/influent/), [Coto on Noun Town](https://cotoacademy.com/noun-town-review-should-you-learn-japanese-in-vr/)). Nobody has shipped a game where sentences and situations, not nouns, are the unit of content. That gap is the opportunity.
2. **The two best-received dedicated games (Wagotabi, Chants of Sennaar) both gate the world behind comprehension.** In Wagotabi NPCs speak only Japanese you have already learned, and new words appear exactly where a task needs them ([Satori Reader](https://blog.satorireader.com/2024/10/11/wagotabi-a-retro-rpg-for-learning-japanese)). In Chants of Sennaar every door, lever and conversation is a translation problem ([Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/chants-of-sennaar)). "Need to understand in order to act" is the mechanic that works; "learn a word, get a star" is the one that does not.
3. **Decipherment is fun when guesses are cheap, feedback is fast, and confirmation is batched.** Chants of Sennaar validates 3-4 glyphs at a time against pictures; Heaven's Vault lets you carry provisional readings for hours. But Emily Short's critique of Heaven's Vault (too rigid, "I could read it but the game would not let me") and So to Speak's playtest finding (delayed confirmation frustrated testers of a *real* language) both say the same thing: for a real language, confirm quickly and let the player be approximately right ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/07/23/heavens-vault-inkle/), [So to Speak dev story](https://hitsave.org/from-phd-to-pixel-art-the-six-year-journey-behind-so-to-speak/)).
4. **Content cost is the thing that kills these projects.** Koe took 10 years from Kickstarter to a "Part 1" with 16 Steam reviews; So to Speak took six solo years for 650 words; Pavel Piezo's postmortem says every phrase multiplies across translation, recording and revision ([Kickstarter](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/297265509/koe-a-jrpg-with-japanese-at-the-core-of-gameplay), [Game Developer postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/postmortem-quot-pavel-piezo-trip-to-the-kite-festival-quot-a-game-for-learning-languages)). A Sims-style game with hand-written dialogue for every domain is the same trap unless dialogue is generated or heavily templated.
5. **Players love games that "happen to be in the language" when text is self-paced, everyday, and repetitive by design.** Animal Crossing and Pokémon top every learner list because villagers reuse stock lines, objects are real things, and nothing is timed ([Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-learning-resources-database/animal-crossing-pocket-camp/), [WaniKani thread](https://community.wanikani.com/t/playing-video-games-in-japanese-is-possible-but-not-fun/50343)). Fantasy vocabulary, auto-scrolling text and real-time action are the stated reasons learners quit.
6. **The failure mode of commercial games as immersion is the lookup tax.** "After an hour I had not finished the intro" (Spiritfarer in Japanese). The fix learners converge on is: only look up recurring words, infer the rest, and pick content you already partly understand ([WaniKani thread](https://community.wanikani.com/t/playing-video-games-in-japanese-is-possible-but-not-fun/50343)). A game that controls its own vocabulary can remove that tax entirely, which no commercial game can.
7. **Known-word tracking is a solved UX pattern; copy LingQ/jpdb, not Duolingo.** Blue = new, yellow fading to clear = learning, 0-5 familiarity scale, difficulty of content computed against the learner's known set ([Refold on LingQ](https://refold.la/blog/lingq-review), [jpdb discussion](https://reddit.rtrace.io/r/LearnJapanese/comments/1biyipl/switching_from_anki_to_jpdbio_has_drastically)). Complaints are about counting accuracy in CJK scripts and clunky UI, not the concept. For Mandarin, word segmentation quality will be a first-class problem.
8. **Comprehensible-input purists (Dreaming Spanish) prove a market exists for "no translation ever."** Levels chosen by "understand most of it," hours tracked, no drills ([Dreaming method](https://www.dreaming.com/method)). But even they rely on visual context and slow speech at the super-beginner level; a game's advantage is that it can make the visual context interactive.
9. **AI conversation apps show the ceiling and the pitfalls of generated dialogue.** Praised for low-stakes speaking; criticised for not levelling to the learner, speech misrecognition, switching language unexpectedly, and plateauing after 6-8 weeks ([Praktika Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/praktika.ai), [EngVarta on Speak](https://engvarta.com/speak-app-honest-review-for-indian-learners-vs-live-tutoring/)). If we generate NPC lines, constraining them to the player's known vocabulary is the differentiator, and the hardest part.
10. **"Chocolate-covered broccoli" is the academic consensus on why edutainment fails; the proposed cure is "learning in order to play," not "playing in order to learn."** The game must need the language, the way Chants needs its glyphs ([Reinhardt colloquium listing](https://si.arizona.edu/events/2025/jonathan-s-reinhardt), [IJLTER systematic review](https://ijlter.org/index.php/ijlter/article/view/9828)). Side evidence: Crystallize's forced-cooperation version produced the most learning *and* the most enjoyment ([Cornell](https://news.cornell.edu/node/286131)).

"The Sims in a foreign language" is a recurring idea rather than a product. Ranalli (2008) ran The Sims with supplementary materials in a university ESL class and found statistically significant vocabulary gains ([Iowa State](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/handle/20.500.12876/23657)); Rutgers assigned The Sims 2 as French extra credit ([Rutgers](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/games-people-play-and-languages-which-people-play-them)); learners on forums credit it with household/emotion/activity vocabulary ([Substack comment](https://studynotepad.substack.com/p/actually-using-my-target-languages/comments)). I found no Reddit thread of substance **[unverified]**. The point for us: the Sims domain is already validated as useful vocabulary, but Sims dialogue is Simlish, so the actual language exposure is menus and object names. A Sims-like with real spoken/written dialogue is unoccupied territory.

The common failures are segmentation in CJK, inaccurate "known" counts, and paywalling the ordering/difficulty controls that make the system work.

## 4. Commercial games as immersion material

**What learners recommend and why.** Across WaniKani, Bunpro, Coto and Kanadojo lists the same titles recur: Animal Crossing, Pokémon, Yo-kai Watch, Dragon Quest XI S, Stardew Valley, and fully voiced visual novels ([Kanadojo](https://kanadojo.com/academy/learn-japanese-video-games-guide), [Coto Academy](https://cotoacademy.com/learning-japanese-through-video-games-resources/), [WaniKani recommendations](https://community.wanikani.com/t/video-game-recommendations-for-reading-japanese/63307)). The reasons given:

- **Everyday setting**: Animal Crossing furniture and items "are real things"; villagers of one personality share stock lines so vocabulary recurs ([Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-learning-resources-database/animal-crossing-pocket-camp/), [WaniKani](https://community.wanikani.com/t/practicing-japanese-with-animal-crossing-while-preparing-for-the-update-or-lets-freak-over-the-update/54007)). Yo-kai Watch is praised for "contemporary Japanese that avoids the unusual words common in fantasy".
- **Self-paced text**: press-to-continue dialogue, a backlog, no timers. Visual novels win because "the reader sets the pace".
- **Reading aids**: furigana, voice acting, downloadable scripts, texthooker compatibility. Game Gengo's top-50 list tracks exactly these features ([spreadsheet thread](https://community.wanikani.com/t/spreadsheet-gamegengos-top-50-video-games-for-immersionlearning/56960); his criteria are paraphrased second-hand, **[not read: the video itself]**).
- **Known plot**: play once in English, then in the target language.

**Why some games are too hard**: fantasy and period vocabulary (Persona 5's social links "overwhelming" while battles are easy because vocabulary repeats; P5 "a much bigger step up than P4"); auto-scrolling text (Dragon Quest XI lacks furigana and scrolls); hiragana-only text is counter-intuitively harder because there are no word boundaries; fast action games where you cannot pause ([WaniKani thread](https://community.wanikani.com/t/games-that-are-good-for-japanese-practice/38494?page=6), [Bunpro](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/japanese-gaming/166479?page=4)). The canonical complaint thread, "Playing video games in Japanese is possible but not fun", has the learner give up on Spiritfarer after an hour of dictionary lookups; the consensus reply is "videogames are not a medium you can force yourself through" and "stop looking everything up" ([WaniKani](https://community.wanikani.com/t/playing-video-games-in-japanese-is-possible-but-not-fun/50343)).

For a French/Mandarin design the mapping is direct: Sims-domain vocabulary (home, shop, work, sport) is the Animal Crossing sweet spot; dialogue must be self-paced and repetitive-by-design; and pinyin/furigana-equivalents should be a toggle, not forced.

## 5. Games where deciphering IS the mechanic

| Game | Mechanic | What makes it fun | Link to real language learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Chants of Sennaar](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1931770/Chants_of_Sennaar/) (2023; 98% of 13,366 Steam reviews; NY Game Awards Best Indie) | Five logographic languages; journal of glyph guesses; pages of 3-4 glyphs validated against pictures; bilingual signs/terminals let you compare word order, negation, plurals | Every puzzle, door and NPC is gated on understanding; gentle curve; the languages were designed "as puzzle elements like any others", not linguistically ([devs via GoNintendo](https://www.gonintendo.com/contents/31644-chants-of-sennaar-devs-explain-how-the-game-s-fictional-languages-were-developed)) | A linguistics blogger compares it to the Linguistics Olympiad and suggests playing it localised into an unfamiliar language to pick up vocabulary ([The Language Closet](https://thelanguagecloset.com/2024/12/07/playing-chants-of-sennaar/)). | [Heaven's Vault](https://emshort.blog/2019/07/23/heavens-vault-inkle/) (2019) | Ancient built from primitives (noun/verb/tense markers); provisional translations carried across the game; crossword-like | Each inscription deepens the whole system; replay reveals more | Emily Short caught herself thinking in Greek while decoding; but Ancient is "encoded English", and the mechanic is too rigid late on. | [Tunic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunic_(video_game)) (2022) | Manual pages in an untranslated script; the manual is "for the player, not the fox" | Obtuseness as the feature; community decoded the alphabet | None claimed; shows players will tolerate total opacity if the world rewards it |
| [Her Story](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/her-story-10-years) / [Return of the Obra Dinn](https://kotaku.com/why-obra-dinn-is-one-of-the-years-best-games-1830749972) | Search terms as hypotheses; "one big logic puzzle" where every deduction is a commitment | Player-driven, no hand-holding, familiar input ("anyone who can use Google") | None; design lesson is that deduction is fun when the game withholds confirmation but lets you act on hunches |

Synthesis for our design: decipherment games are fun because (1) comprehension is instrumentally necessary, (2) the hypothesis space is small at any moment (3-4 glyphs, one word), (3) confirmation is delayed just long enough to feel earned, (4) the world is consistent so inference is possible. The warnings from Short and from So to Speak's testers are that with a *real* language players want faster, more forgiving confirmation, and that "encoded English" leaks through when the designer does not respect the target language's own structure (critical for Mandarin).

## 6. Why most language-learning games fail

- **Chocolate-covered broccoli.** Granic, Lobel & Engels (2014) as quoted in a DGBLL review: such games "look great, they are good for you, but they ultimately fail to work because the creative game dynamics that induce transportation and immersion are missing" ([Etnolingwistyka review](https://etnolingwistyka.journals.umcs.pl/ff/article/download/17120/12018)). The metaphor dates to Bruckman (1999) and the 1980s edutainment wave ([SciTePress](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2017/63528/pdf/index.html)). Reinhardt's prescription: "learning in order to play", not "playing in order to learn" ([Arizona colloquium listing](https://si.arizona.edu/events/2025/jonathan-s-reinhardt); abstract **[not read: page redirected]**). The Joan Ganz Cooney Center traces the 1990s edutainment collapse to consolidation and price pressure that favoured volume over quality ([EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/news/2016-02-07-is-the-educational-games-industry-falling-into-the-same-trap-it-did-20-years-ago)).
- **Thin evidence base, but positive where it exists.** A 2023 systematic review of 18 studies (83% serious games) finds positive effects on contextualised vocabulary, oral skills and grammar, and that narrative, goals, rewards and feedback are the elements studies credit ([IJLTER](https://ijlter.org/index.php/ijlter/article/view/9828), abstract only). An earlier review found only 26 of hundreds of CALL papers in 15 years concerned games. A 2026 Bayesian meta-analysis of game-enhanced vocabulary learning exists but I could not read results ([JCAL via MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/122315-a-bayesian-meta-analysis-of-digital-game-enhanced-vocabulary-learning)) **[not read]**.
- **Content cost and scope.** Pavel Piezo (two people, 150 objects, 4 languages, 7,500 phrases): translation "could not be one-to-one", phrases had to be re-done once translators saw the art, game shipped three levels short ([postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/postmortem-quot-pavel-piezo-trip-to-the-kite-festival-quot-a-game-for-learning-languages)). So to Speak: two-year plan became six; engine rewrite; 650 words. Koe: ten years to Part 1. Lingotopia: 18 months for a game reviewers called thin. The vocabulary ceiling of every shipped title is 400-1,000 words, which is below A2. No independent postmortem of a commercially *failed* language game was found **[unverified gap]**; the Lingotopia and Influent devs did not publish one.
- **Retention.** Duolingo, the best-funded product in the category, retains ~12% of installs at D30 and ~7% at D365 ([Udonis/AppMagic](https://www.blog.udonis.co/mobile-marketing/mobile-apps/duolingo)); its lever is the streak, not the content. Games that are "quizzes in a world" inherit app-style churn without app-style notifications.
- **Fun vs pedagogy, as learners state it.** "It stops being fun for me if I always have to keep looking everything up" and "you do have to have the patience with going slower than you'd want to" ([WaniKani](https://community.wanikani.com/t/playing-video-games-in-japanese-is-possible-but-not-fun/50343)). The Crystallize result that forced cooperation raised both learning and enjoyment is the only data point I found where the two moved together, and it came from making the language *socially* necessary ([Cornell](https://news.cornell.edu/node/286131)).
- **Reddit consensus** (as far as it can be sourced from forum mirrors): dedicated games are good for kana or a first few hundred words, then you move to real media with Yomitan/Anki; nobody reports reaching conversational ability from a game alone **[informal, unverified]**.

# Immersion and comprehensible-input theory: what constrains the game

Scope: the AJATT / MIA-Refold / Krashen-ALG-Dreaming Spanish school, the lexical-coverage and extensive-reading literature underneath it, French- and Mandarin-specific coverage facts, SRS / i+1 tooling, and what these communities say about video games as immersion material. Sources are linked inline. Items marked **[unverified]** or **[secondary]** could not be checked against a primary source within this session (web search budget ran out; several primary pages are dead or paywalled).

## Summary: the facts that most constrain the design

1. **The 95% / 98% rule.** Reading research converges on 98% of running words known (1 unknown in 50) for "adequate" unassisted comprehension, with 95% (1 in 20) as a minimum. Comprehension rises roughly linearly with coverage: ~50% comprehension at 90% coverage, ~60% at 95%, ~68% at 98%, ~75% at 100% ([Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf); [Hu & Nation 2000 summarised in Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)). There is no cliff; every extra percent of known words buys ~2 points of comprehension.
2. **Viewing with pictures needs less coverage than reading.** With video, 95% coverage was "optimal" and comprehension held up even at 80%; 90% vs 95% was not significantly different ([Durbahn et al. 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/lexical-coverage-in-l1-and-l2-viewing-comprehension/DFCA6605076705D5762C98F286D16B27)). Listening alone: 95% may suffice and is reachable with 2,000–3,000 word families (van Zeeland & Schmitt 2012, cited [here](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)). A Sims-style game with strong visual grounding sits in the "viewing" regime, so it can run at ~95% rather than 98%.
3. **Spoken/domestic language is lexically small; text is not.** In Taiwan Mandarin conversation the top 1,000 words cover 84.4% of tokens, 2,000 cover 89.9%, 3,000 cover 92.5%, 5,000 cover 95.3%, 10,000 cover 98.4%; in the written Sinica corpus 2,000 words cover only 68.7% ([Tseng 2013](https://aclanthology.org/O13-2001.pdf)). In French novels 2,000 lemmas cover ~91%, 5,000 ~96.5%, and 7,000–9,000 are needed for 98% ([Ramnäs 2019](https://bells.uib.no/index.php/bells/article/download/1430/2856)). A home/shop/work/sport sim should stay in the conversational register, where ~3,000 words gets ~92–95%.
4. **Words need ~8–12+ meaningful encounters to stick.** Fewer than 8 encounters in a graded reader gave ~0 retained meanings after 3 months; even 15–18 encounters gave only ~50% form recognition at 3 months ([Waring & Takaki 2003](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf)). Nation uses 12 repetitions as the planning minimum ([Nation 2014](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1044345.pdf)). The game must schedule re-encounters, not just first encounters.
5. **Repetition cost grows steeply with rank.** To meet most words of the 2nd 1,000 twelve times takes ~171k running words; the 3rd 1,000 needs ~300k; the 5th ~1.06M; the 9th ~2.96M ([Nation 2014, Table 1](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1044345.pdf)). Natural text alone cannot deliver mid-frequency words often enough; narrow domains (or deliberate scripting) are how practitioners fix this.
6. **Krashen says don't aim at i+1 deliberately; practitioners do it anyway.** Krashen's own text: if input is understood and there is enough of it, "i+1 will be provided automatically", and deliberately targeting i+1 "may even be harmful" ([Principles and Practice, pp. 20–21](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf)). The engineering school (MorphMan, jpdb, Migaku) ignores this and explicitly selects "1T" sentences with exactly one unknown.
7. **Comprehension is per-content, not per-learner.** Refold measures comprehension against a specific piece of content ("level 5 on a children's show but level 3 on the news") and gates progress on reaching level 5 in one *domain* ([Refold levels of comprehension](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/levels-of-comprehension); [Refold 2A/2C search summary](https://refold.la/?p=53467)). Refold explicitly refuses percentage self-ratings because "people are generally bad at estimating percentages". A game can measure coverage objectively, which no existing method can.
8. **Output is deferred by everyone in this school, for hundreds of hours.** Refold starts speaking in Phase 4 after "hundreds of hours" ([Refold: starting output](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-3/a/starting-output)); Dreaming Spanish recommends speaking at Level 6 (~1,000 h) and reading at Level 5–6 ([Dreaming Spanish FAQ](https://www.dreaming.com/faq)); ALG keeps students silent for "several hundred hours" ([ALG in a few words](https://www.dreaming.com/blog-posts/alg-method-in-a-few-words)). A text-only-input game is squarely orthodox for this school; it should not force the player to produce.
9. **Time budgets are large.** Dreaming Spanish's ladder: 50 h / 150 h / 300 h / 600 h / 1,000 h / 1,500 h for levels 2–7, halved for speakers of a related language, roughly doubled for an unrelated one ([palteca reproduction](https://palteca.com/dreaming-spanish-review) **[secondary]**; [official halving rule](https://www.dreaming.com/blog-posts/the-og-immersion-method)). Refold estimates Phases 1–2 at 220 h (cousin language) to 750 h (distant language) ([Refold Phase 2](https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-2)). Mandarin for an English speaker is the "distant" case.
10. **Mandarin's hard part is the script, and the CI community has no settled answer.** 1,000 characters cover 89% of modern Chinese text, 1,500 cover 94.6%, 2,000 cover 97.1%, 2,500 cover 98.5%, 3,000 cover 99.2% ([Jun Da corpus](https://lingua.mtsu.edu/chinese-computing/statistics/char/list.php?Which=MO)), but character coverage overstates word comprehension because most words are multi-character compounds. Krashen himself argues for a pinyin-first stage ([The Case for Pinyin, 2021](https://engagedscholarship.csuohio.edu/cltmt/vol4/iss2/2)); Refold says characters come from repeated exposure in content, with a pop-up dictionary as the crutch ([Refold Mandarin](https://refold.la/how-to-learn-mandarin/)).

## 1. AJATT (All Japanese All The Time, Khatzumoto)

The original site (alljapaneseallthetime.com) is offline; this section relies on **[secondary]** reconstructions: a [learnanylanguage wiki entry](https://learnanylanguage.fandom.com/wiki/All_Japanese_All_The_Time) (paywalled on fetch), a detailed [Russian write-up of the 10,000 sentences method](https://www.ixbt.com/live/comments/3108083), a [retrospective by a former follower](https://korekara.beehiiv.com/p/learned-khatzumoto-founder-ajatt), and a [WaniKani community debate](https://community.wanikani.com/t/ajatt-and-wanikani-timelines/27829).

**Core claims.** Three pillars: attitude (make it fun), a 24/7 all-Japanese environment (replace all L1 media), and an SRS to capture and review sentences. Sequence: Heisig *Remembering the Kanji* first (all ~2,000 kanji as meaning+shape, no readings), then "10,000 sentences", with grammar study rejected in favour of absorbing patterns from whole sentences. Khatzumoto claimed to go from zero (June 2004) to working as a programmer in Tokyo in ~18 months.

**10,000 sentences / sentence mining.** Each card is a full sentence containing something unknown, pulled from media you are already consuming (sub2srs for subtitles). "Knowing" a sentence = read it aloud, understand every word, and (originally) write it by hand. The 10,000 figure was a benchmark, not a derived number; the write-up notes that at 20 sentences/day it is ~500 days of card-making alone. The community later classified sentences as 0T / 1T / 2T (zero, one, two+ unknown "targets") and advised mining only 1T sentences, which is the direct ancestor of MorphMan's i+1. Khatzumoto himself later moved from 10k sentences to "Massive-Context Cloze Deletions" (MCDs: long passages with one blank), a change the community found poorly explained.

**What the community judged.** Worked: immersion itself, enjoyment as the retention mechanism, sentence (not word) cards, native-script-only cards. Didn't: the absolutes ("never output early", "RTK first", "Anki + immersion is all you need") that the author says fail "about 99% of learners"; sentence mining becoming "more of a crutch than a benefit" past ~7,000–10,000 cards; total L1 avoidance as unrealistic and burnout-prone; unverifiable self-reports ("95% of whatever you show him" at month 12, with critics noting reading ability is hard to self-assess without feedback). The 10k figure did not survive; the 1T-sentence idea did.

## 2. MIA / Refold (Matt vs Japan)

Refold is the successor to Matt vs Japan's "Mass Immersion Approach". The site is mid-rewrite: the original 4-stage roadmap (1 Foundation, 2 Comprehension, 3 Output, 4 Mastery) is being replaced by 8 phases (0 Immersion Learning, 1 Foundations, 2 Comprehension, 3 Listening, 4 Speaking, 5 Accuracy, 6 Fluency, 7 And Beyond) ([roadmap overview](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-0/a/roadmap-overview)).

**Stages and numbers.** Phase 1: first 100 words (1A), the most common 1,000 words "make up about 70% of a language" (1C); 40 / 75 / 100 / 150 hours for cousin / similar / neutral / distant languages ([Phase 1](https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-1)). Phase 2: 2A build to "the 2000 most common words", 2B sentence-mine domain-specific vocabulary, 2C move from tool-supported to free-flow reading; 180 / 375 / 400 / 600 hours (cumulative 220 / 450 / 500 / 750) ([Phase 2](https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-2)). Reading leads listening "because you control the pace (pausing, rereading, looking things up)"; listening catches up in Phase 3.

**The input + SRS split.** Refold calls SRS/grammar "priming": it "prepares you to learn [the language] when you encounter it naturally" but "can never replace" acquisition; sessions should be short because of diminishing returns ([0B acquisition vs study](https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-0/0b-acquisition-vs-study)). In the old roadmap Stage 1 cards were TL→NL recognition only, no production.

**Stance on output.** Start speaking in Phase 4 after "hundreds of hours" of comprehension. Rationale: comprehension gives a "built-in error detector"; early production risks fossilisation (citing Han 2004); and conversation on 100 words is demotivating. Case study cited: 1,000 hours of Czech input before first speaking ([starting output](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-3/a/starting-output)).

**How they define "comprehensible".** The original roadmap used six levels against a specific piece of content: ... Level 4 "understand the meaning of most sentences, follow the story but miss some ideas and plot points"; Level 5 "nearly every sentence, occasional word or phrase missed"; Level 6 effortless. The new library replaces this with a 1–10 "rate of perceived exertion" style self-rating of comprehension plus effort, judged against a reference show (Peppa Pig vs Scrubs), and explicitly avoids percentages: "does 70% mean 70% of the words, sentences, or meaning?" ([levels of comprehension](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/levels-of-comprehension)). Gating: level 5 in one domain to leave Stage 2C.

**Tolerance for ambiguity.** Active immersion is "time spent actively trying to understand, even when you don't fully succeed"; mostly-incomprehensible input still has value because the brain is "looking for patterns, testing hypotheses"; early learners "discard much of what you hear" and can only focus "a few minutes at a time" ([0C immerse](https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-0/0c-immerse-in-the-language)). The exact phrase "tolerate ambiguity" was not found on the current site **[unverified]**; TheMoeWay states the same idea bluntly: "Learning a language requires you to be content with not understanding everything" ([learnjapanese.moe guide](https://learnjapanese.moe/guide/)).

**Narrow immersion / domains.** Stage 2C "Master a Domain": pick one genre/series/topic and stay in it until level 5, because vocabulary and style recur within a domain and comprehension is domain-specific. The library's [narrow reading](https://www.refold.la/roadmap/library/narrow-reading) article cites Krashen 2004 (narrow reading builds fluency within a limited vocabulary faster), Nation 2001 (wide reading spreads words thin), Webb 2007 (frequency of encounter is a top predictor of acquisition), and recommends starting a children's/YA series from book one and finishing it.

## 3. Krashen, ALG, Dreaming Spanish

**Krashen.** Input hypothesis: "a necessary (but not sufficient) condition to move from stage i to stage i+1 is that the acquirer understand input that contains i+1, where 'understand' means that the acquirer is focussed on the meaning and not the form" ([Principles and Practice 1982, p. 20](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf)). Understanding beyond competence is possible via "context, our knowledge of the world, our extra-linguistic information". Part 3: input need not contain *only* i+1; "roughly-tuned" input (caretaker speech) is preferable to finely-tuned because it hits i+1 without guesswork and "provides built-in review" (p. 21–22). Speaking "emerges" and "cannot be taught directly"; adults denied a silent period fall back on L1 rules (p. 26–27). Affective filter: anxiety/low motivation means "even if they understand the message, the input will not reach the part of the brain responsible for language acquisition" (p. 31). Standard criticisms: untestable, i+1 undefined operationally ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input_hypothesis)).

**ALG (Marvin Brown, AUA Bangkok).** Two teachers act out and talk about things in plain view; no books; students "don't take notes, don't try to memorize", and don't speak "until words and phrases come to them without conscious effort", which "may take several hundred hours" ([ALG in a few words](https://www.dreaming.com/blog-posts/alg-method-in-a-few-words)). Brown's thesis: "only adults can do it wrong": early speaking and analysis, not age, cause the adult ceiling; AUA students listened "up to a year" and shorter silent periods gave "proportionately less-perfect results" ([Beyond Language Learning](https://beyondlanguagelearning.com/2017/12/05/the-alg-shaped-hole-in-research-on-second-language-acquisition)). Evidence is AUA's own reports, not controlled studies. The "800 hours" figure sometimes quoted was **not found** in any source **[unverified]**.

**Dreaming Spanish.** Levels 1–7; hour thresholds (0 / 50 / 150 / 300 / 600 / 1,000 / 1,500) with approximate known-word counts (0 / 300 / 1,500 / 3,000 / 5,000 / 7,000 / 12,000+) and descriptors ("understand a patient speaker" at L4, "native speakers speaking normally" at L5, "comfortable with daily conversation" at L6) as reproduced by a [third-party review](https://palteca.com/dreaming-spanish-review) **[secondary: the official roadmap is an image]**. Official rules ([OG immersion method](https://www.dreaming.com/blog-posts/the-og-immersion-method); [FAQ](https://www.dreaming.com/faq)): Romance-language speakers divide hours by 2, unrelated-L1 learners roughly double them; no subtitles ("if you need subtitles, move to an easier video"); don't look words up unless a recurring concrete noun; no grammar; flashcards "don't count as input"; reading from Level 5 (ideally 6) to protect pronunciation; speaking at Level 6, needing "a few dozen hours of practice at most" to train the mouth; reading volume targets of ~1M words for "considerable improvement", ~3M to read almost any book. Lower levels should focus on "concrete nouns and common verbs"; abstract vocabulary later.

## 4. Vocabulary coverage, graded readers, incidental acquisition

**Coverage thresholds.** Hu & Nation 2000 (nonsense-word substitution): at 80% coverage no one reached adequate comprehension, at 90% a small minority, at 95% "a few more... still a small minority", at 100% most; regression put the needed level at 98% ([as reported in Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)). Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011 (661 learners, real texts): comprehension 50.5% at 90% coverage, 60.5% at 95%, 68.3% at 98%, 75.3% at 100% (Table 2); "relatively linear... no indication of a vocabulary threshold"; native speakers scored 78% at 100% ([PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)). Carver's L1 finding: ~1% unknown when material matches ability, ~2% when hard.

**Word families implied (English).** For 98%: 8,000–9,000 families for novels/newspapers, 6,000–7,000 for spoken text; the first 2,000 families cover 87.8% of novels and ~83% of newspapers; 4,000 + proper nouns ≈ 95%; the children's film *Shrek* (10k tokens) is 81.5% first-1,000 and reaches 98% with ~3,000 families + proper nouns, but its off-list words are topic words (donkey ×42, ogre ×25, swamp ×20) ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)). TV/movies: ~3,000 families for 95%, ~7,000 for 98% (Webb & Rodgers 2009, cited in [Ha 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.800983/full)). The *Shrek* finding is the design-relevant one: a narrow story's own topic words become high-frequency inside it.

**Graded readers.** Publishers control vocabulary by headword count; the Extensive Reading Foundation scale runs Alphabet (1–50 headwords) → Beginner (51–300) → Elementary (301–800) → Intermediate (801–1,500) → Upper-Intermediate (1,501–2,400) → Advanced (2,401–4,500) → Bridge (4,501–8,000) → Near Native (8,001–18,000) ([ERF scale](https://er-central.com/ERF/ERF_levels.htm)). In practice "i+1" is the 1-in-50 to 1-in-20 unknown density: Waring & Takaki engineered their text to 96.2% known (Hu & Nation's 96–99% for successful guessing) by choosing a 400-headword reader for intermediates.

**Incidental acquisition rates.** Waring & Takaki 2003 (25 substitute words in a 400-headword reader, n=15): immediate meaning-translation 4.6/25, falling to 1.9 at one week and 0.9 at three months; form recognition 15.3 → 11.1 → 8.4; words met 15–18 times scored 4.2/5 immediately and 2.3/5 at three months; words met once were ~0.5/5. Conclusion: ~one retained word per hour of reading, nothing retained below 8 encounters, "a massive amount of graded reading is needed" ([PDF](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf)). Nation 2014 adopts 12 repetitions as "moderately safe" (Webb 2007 and Vidal 2011 agree; Waring & Takaki and Brown et al. 2008 say it may be too few) and computes the running words needed per 1,000-family band (171k / 300k / 535k / 1.06M / 1.45M / 2.04M / 2.43M / 2.96M for bands 2–9), i.e. ~3M tokens to meet the first 9,000 families 12 times, or at 200 wpm, 21 min/week at band 2 rising to 6 h 10 min/week at band 9 ([PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1044345.pdf)). Krashen's narrow-reading argument (same author/topic → fewer types, more repeats, "first few pages" effect) is the practitioner-side answer ([Krashen 2004 summary](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/2018_potential_of_series_books.pdf)).

## 5. French and Mandarin specifics

**French.** Lonsdale & Le Bras's frequency dictionary ranks the top 5,000 lemmas from a 23M-word mixed written/spoken corpus but does not publish coverage percentages ([Routledge](https://resourcecentre.routledge.com/books/9780415775311)). Ramnäs 2019, using those lists on three contemporary French novels: 1,000 lemmas ≈ 85–86%, 2,000 ≈ 90–91.5%, 3,000 ≈ 93–94%, 4,000 ≈ 95–96%, 5,000 ≈ 96–97%, 7,000 ≈ 97.4–98.0%, 9,000 ≈ 98.1–98.5%; so "7,000 to 9,000 lemmas will provide the 98% coverage" ([PDF](https://bells.uib.no/index.php/bells/article/download/1430/2856)). Note the unit is the lemma, not the word family (families are ~22% fewer). Spoken French: the 1950s *Français fondamental* 1er degré list is 1,475 words derived from 312k words of recorded conversation plus "availability" criteria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ais_fondamental)); no coverage figure found **[unverified]**. CEFR: the French Threshold (B1) wordlist is just under 2,000 words, A2 ~1,000; Milton estimates the top 2,000 words give ~80% coverage of "normal text" ([LLAS paper](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/www.llas.ac.uk/resources/paper/2715), fetch blocked, quoted via search snippet **[secondary]**).

**Mandarin: words.** Taiwan Mandarin Conversational Corpus (405k tokens, 16.7k types): 1,000 words → 84.4%, 2,000 → 89.9%, 3,000 → 92.5%, 4,000 → 94.2%, 5,000 → 95.3%, 7,000 → 97.0%, 10,000 → 98.4%; the same 2,000 words cover only 68.7% of the written Sinica corpus (which needs 15,000 types for 90%). The conversational top-2,000 use just 1,065 distinct characters and 654 tone-specified syllables; 300 tone-syllables cover 90% of conversation ([Tseng 2013](https://aclanthology.org/O13-2001.pdf)). SUBTLEX-CH (33.5M words of subtitles) is the usual proxy for "everyday exposure" ([PLoS ONE 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/20532192)).

**Mandarin: characters.** Jun Da's modern corpus (~193M characters): rank 500 → 75.8%, 1,000 → 89.1%, 1,500 → 94.6%, 2,000 → 97.1%, 2,500 → 98.5%, 3,000 → 99.2%, 3,500 → 99.5% ([list](https://lingua.mtsu.edu/chinese-computing/statistics/char/list.php?Which=MO)). Character coverage ≠ word coverage: at 1,000 characters (~90% character coverage) learners read only ~70% of common multi-character words ([search summary of learner sites](https://www.newconceptmandarin.com/learn-chinese-blog/many-chinese-characters-need-learn/) **[secondary]**).

**HSK 3.0 (2021).** Bands 1–9: new words 500 / 772 / 973 / 1,000 / 1,071 / 1,140 / 5,636 (bands 7–9 combined), cumulative 500 / 1,272 / 2,245 / 3,245 / 4,316 / 5,456 / 11,092; recognition characters 300 per band to 1,800 at band 6, 3,000 total; writing characters 300 / 700 / 1,200 by stage; grammar points 48 → 572 cumulative ([Wikipedia table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi)). HSK 3.0 band 3 (2,245 words) roughly matches the "2,000 words ≈ 90% of conversation" point above.

**Why Mandarin is harder and how CI people handle the script.** Tones change meaning; characters carry no reliable phonetic cue, so listening and reading are separate acquisitions. Krashen's 2021 proposal: give beginners comprehensible *pinyin* reading to build oral Mandarin first, which then makes hanzi "more predictable and easier" ([The Case for Pinyin](https://engagedscholarship.csuohio.edu/cltmt/vol4/iss2/2)); counter-evidence: pinyin shown during word learning can interfere with later character recognition ([JCU study](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/39120/)). Refold's Mandarin guide: tones are picked up "by listening to real Mandarin, not by drilling tone charts"; characters are recognised "through repeated exposure in content you care about"; its 1K deck shows simplified characters with pinyin on every word and sentence; recommended crutches are a pop-up dictionary and dual subtitles ([Refold Mandarin](https://refold.la/how-to-learn-mandarin/)). A Refold learner's post reports starting with gaming content "because it's very visual and you don't really have to understand what's being said". The Mandarin CI community has not converged on pinyin-first vs characters-first **[contested]**.

## 6. SRS, sentence mining, and i+1 selection in practice

**Role and critiques.** AJATT made Anki central; Refold demotes it to "priming" with short sessions; Dreaming Spanish/ALG reject it ("flashcards don't count as input"). Practitioner critiques: cards past ~7k–10k become a crutch; isolated-word SRS is poor for beginners who need many new words ([Tae Kim review](https://www.guidetojapanese.org/blog/?p=545)); TheMoeWay caps new cards at 20/day (10 if too many), uses the ~1,500-word Kaishi deck, and pivots to mining from immersion after it ([guide](https://learnjapanese.moe/guide/)).

**Operational definitions.** A "1T" / i+1 sentence = every word known except one. MorphMan (Anki add-on) parses cards into morphemes, tracks which are known (by card maturity, or by imported known-word lists), scores each new card by its count of unknown (k+N) and immature (m+N) morphemes, marks the single unknown as the "focus morph", and reorders the new-card queue so i+1 cards come first; a frequency list can prioritise which unknown to teach next; Chinese is supported via Jieba segmentation or a per-character CJK morphemizer ([README](https://github.com/dmgerman/MorphMan); [wiki](https://github.com/kaegi/MorphMan/wiki/I-plus-1)). jpdb.io has per-work decks (anime, novels, games) with a known/learning/never-forget state per word, deck "% words known" coverage, an ML difficulty rating trained on crowdsourced pairwise comparisons, and auto-served i+1 example sentences ([FAQ](https://jpdb.io/faq); [Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/jpdb/about)). Migaku assigns Unknown / Known / Learning / Tracking / Ignored to every word and computes a per-video "comprehension score" by comparing content words to known words, and can auto-pause Netflix on 1T sentences whose unknown word is above a frequency threshold ([Migaku](https://migaku.com/learn-french); [Bunpro thread](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/whats-your-experience-with-sentence-mining/49802)). Community rule of thumb: SRS the words that make up 95% of a show's tokens and the rest is learnable from context ([WaniKani thread](https://community.wanikani.com/t/getting-started-with-jpbd/58352)).

Adjacent: Duolingo/Stanford's CALM paper shows LLM output can be held to a target CEFR level via fine-tuning + RL, an existing approach for generating level-controlled text ([Malik et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.925/) **[URL unverified; PDF read locally]**).

## 7. Video games as immersion material

Found no Refold-authored analysis of *why* games work (the relevant blog post is gone). Practitioner claims come from Game Gengo, TheMoeWay, Migaku's guide and forum threads.

- **Game Gengo** (Matt, YouTube/Patreon): teaches the full JLPT vocab/kanji/grammar through games because they teach "through context rather than stale-textbooks" and give "real, natural Japanese"; makes per-game guided-immersion material and Anki decks ([Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/GameGengo/about)).
- **TheMoeWay**: games and visual novels are valid immersion from the start; VNs "mix literary prose and conversational Japanese", are read for hours when novels tire the reader, and are tooled with Textractor → texthooker page → Yomitan pop-up dictionary → one-click Anki cards ([VN guide](https://learnjapanese.moe/vn/)); milestones include "1 and 2 medium-to-long visual novels" ([guide](https://learnjapanese.moe/guide/)).
- **Migaku's games guide**: interactivity (choices depend on understanding, so you are "actively problem-solving with the language"); repetition (Pokémon battle commands learned in the first few battles; "about 20% of a game's vocabulary appears 80% of the time"); visual grounding (seeing an item and its effect "sticks better than a flashcard"); independent text/audio language settings; ability to re-read on-screen text after mishearing. Genre ladder: Pokémon / Animal Crossing / Stardew Valley for beginners, narrative RPGs for intermediates, VNs and strategy games for advanced; life sims (The Sims, Animal Crossing) recommended specifically for "conversation and everyday vocabulary". Caveats: too-hard games cause quitting, one genre gives vocabulary gaps, game-specific slang ([Migaku](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/language-learning-with-video-games)).
- **Forum experience**: fully voiced games with text logs give listening + re-readable text; furigana and dialogue logs let you revisit lines; players still look up many words, so games are not a shortcut ([Bunpro](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/japanese-gaming/166479?page=2); [WaniKani](https://community.wanikani.com/t/game-gengo-n4-grammar-overview/51016)).
- **Research**: visual novels studied as a multimodal medium with motivation/vocabulary gains ([Illinois thesis](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/118567)); Newcombe & Brick 2017 find in-game learning opportunities "highly varied" and needing scaffolding ([IGI](https://ideas.repec.org/a/igg/jcallt/v7y2017i4p75-89.html)). The explicit "text speed under player control" argument appears in Refold's reasoning for reading-before-listening ("you control the pace") rather than in a games-specific source.
